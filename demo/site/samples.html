What the simulated camera sees

Every picture on this page is a frame rendered by the simulator, just as it would arrive from a locked 16-bit camera, shrunk for the web. Each comes with its ground truth: where each projector's light really lands and how far apart the two put the same content. The detector will never see that truth; it is drawn on top here so you can tell a projector's edge from an edge in the content.

The installation

A lecture-hall screen, two projectors, one camera

Projector A lights the left part of the screen and projector B the right; in the middle, the overlap, both shine and the calibration software fades one out as the other fades in. Printed markers on the frame around the screen (the bezel) let the camera work out exactly where the screen is.

Technical details
  • Screen mm, 0.9 reflectance, room light 0.02 of projector white (a dim lecture hall), a 150 mm bezel with 8 ArUco markers (DICT_4X4_50, 80 mm).
  • Two 1920 × 1080 projectors, each image 2000 mm wide (1.04 mm per pixel); a 410 mm overlap; B sits 0.3 mm lower so the two pixel grids never line up. Cosine blend in linear light; black level 1/1500 of white.
  • Camera px, mono, 16-bit linear, whole screen plus a 5% margin with a 2% keystone (about 0.87 camera px per mm); exposure 1/30 s; one frame every 0.5 s.
  • Toggle shadows boosted to see the frame through a log curve: the faint glow of each projector's black level around the picture becomes visible.

How a frame is made

Misalignment

From invisible to obvious

The same moment of the same lecture, with projector B moved across the overlap by 0 to 8 of its pixels. Everything else is identical, down to the camera noise, because the variants of a sweep share their random draws. Pick a size.

Technical details

Scenario shift_sweep, frame (t = s): B moved at 615 s, after the 600 s trusted window. One projector pixel is mm. The difference map shows |frame − aligned twin| at full colour from electrons (a quarter of one projector's white), on a dimmed copy of the frame.

Where A shines alone the difference is exactly zero: the largest change there is electrons. Where B shines alone, B's whole picture moved: nobody can see that, because nothing there is compared with anything. It shows only in the overlap, and at B's outer edge.

Arrangements

Any overlap shape

The projectors may sit side by side, one above the other, at an angle, meeting at a corner, at different sizes, or mostly on top of each other. The overlap is never assumed: it is the intersection of the two boxes, whatever shape that is. The colour strip under each frame shows who lights each point: A, B, and the mix where the blend shares them.

Content

Whatever is on the screen

The check has to work during real lectures, whatever is on screen. Each kind of frame is useful for something. Textured regions show doubled contours. Flat regions show brightness changes. Dark regions show each projector's outline. Frames with motion or a cut are skipped, because a camera exposure that spans two video frames already looks doubled.

Technical details: the routing thresholds

CLAUDE.md §4.3 routes each region by its mean brightness and edge density: dark below DARK_LEVEL ( of white), and skip when the mean frame difference exceeds MOTION_LEVEL ( of white). Each card lists the overlap's mean brightness as the camera measures it, then with the room light taken away, and, for moving content, the mean change from the previous frame. The badge applies those two thresholds. Textured versus flat needs a per-tile edge density, which phase 3 will define, so other content keeps the kind it was chosen for. With the room at 0.02 of white, even a black frame measures about 0.021 of white, so the badges judge "dark" after taking the room light away. Even then, neither dark-film frame counts as dark, and the moving one changes far too little to be skipped. Both are open questions for phase 3; see the findings on the tests page.

Black frames

What the camera sees in the dark

A projector's black still leaks a little light, about 1/1500 of its white. In a black frame that leak outlines each projector's full box, even the edges hidden under the other projector, and the overlap glows twice as bright. It is faint, about electrons on top of from the room light. Averaging frames brings it out.

Room light removed, then stretched from to electrons. Frames – of shift_sweep, during its black interlude.

Technical details

Per-pixel noise is electrons in one frame and in the mean of twelve; the profile averages along the screen's height as well. Levels in the mean: unlit , A only , overlap , B only electrons. This is the raw material of boundary analysis.

Nuisances

Things that must never trigger a YES

Real rooms are untidy. The camera gets knocked, a lamp ages, someone switches the lights on, people walk past, projectors flicker, a camera leaves its sharpening on. None of these moves a projector, so the truth stays aligned, and the detector must keep answering NO.

Screens and cameras

A gain screen, and a camera zoomed on the overlap

A gain screen sends more light back in one direction, so each projector shows a hotspot where its light reflects toward the camera, and the overlap falls between the two. That fixed shading must not pass for misalignment. A zoomed camera sees only the overlap, its control strips and four markers, at twice the resolution.

Ground truth

What each scenario asks the detector to notice

For every frame the simulator records the true offset: how far apart A and B now put the same piece of content, at the worst point of the overlap. These timelines come straight from that record.

Rotations: same offset, different angle

Turning B about its centre or about its far corner. An offset at the overlap needs half the angle when the pivot is twice as far away.
